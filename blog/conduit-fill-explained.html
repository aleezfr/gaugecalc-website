Conduit Fill Explained: How Many Wires Fit in a Conduit

What the fill limits really mean, how to read the NEC tables, and how to size EMT for a real pull, with two worked examples.

By Ali Zafar · Last updated: October 2026

Why fill limits exist

A conduit that is stuffed too full is hard to pull, tends to scrape insulation, and traps heat. The NEC limits how much of the conduit's cross-section the wires may take up. The rules are simple once you know three numbers and where the areas come from. The Conduit Fill Calculator does the arithmetic for you; this guide explains what it is doing.

Step 1: Count every conductor

Add up each wire that will sit in the conduit: hots, neutrals, and equipment grounding conductors. A common slip is to count only the current-carrying wires and forget the ground. Every conductor takes up space, so every one counts toward fill.

Step 2: Pick the fill limit

Conductors in the conduitLimit
153%
231%
3 or more40%

Three or more wires is the usual case. The 31% figure for two wires is lower because two similar wires can lock together side by side and jam during the pull.

Step 3: Add up the wire areas

Each size has a published area that includes the insulation. For common THHN/THWN-2 building wire, 14 AWG is 0.0097 sq in, 12 AWG is 0.0133 sq in and 10 AWG is 0.0211 sq in. Multiply each area by the number of wires of that size and add everything together. Thicker insulation types, such as XHHW, have different areas.

Step 4: Find a conduit with enough area

Multiply the inside area of each conduit size by the fill limit and look for the first size where your total wire area fits. For EMT, 1/2" is 0.304 sq in inside (0.122 sq in at 40%), 3/4" is 0.533 sq in (0.213 at 40%), and 1" is 0.864 sq in (0.346 at 40%). Other conduit types such as PVC or rigid have different internal areas.

Worked example: a small branch circuit run

Seven 12 AWG and three 10 AWG THHN wires, including grounds. The total is 7 × 0.0133 + 3 × 0.0211 = 0.1564 sq in. With ten wires the limit is 40%. 1/2" EMT allows only 0.122 sq in, so it is too small. 3/4" allows 0.213 sq in, so the pull fits at about 29% fill. That is the answer, with room to spare.

Leave yourself some slack

Passing the table does not mean the pull will be easy. Long runs, several bends, or wires that are stiff and large make the pull harder, so many installers go up one trade size when the fill is near the limit. A larger conduit also leaves room to add a circuit later.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Not counting grounding conductors.
  • Using THHN areas for a different insulation type.
  • Using 40% for two wires instead of 31%.
  • Forgetting that many current-carrying wires in one conduit also need ampacity derating. See the wire size guide.
  • Treating conduit fill as a substitute for checking bends, pull tension and the code edition in force.
